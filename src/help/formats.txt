
TIMESTAMP FORMATS
RFC3339 (read/written by -s/--split, -u/--utc, -l/--local,
-o/--offset, and -d/--duration when pointed at a timestamp
column): a specific, common way of writing a date, time, and UTC
offset together, e.g.

|    2024-06-15T14:00:00+02:00
|    2024-06-15T14:00:00Z          (Z means UTC, same as +00:00)

An offset missing its colon (+0200 instead of +02:00) is also
accepted when reading, even though it isn't strictly valid
RFC3339. A space where the 'T' belongs (2024-06-15 14:00:00Z) is
accepted when reading too, which RFC3339 allows for readability
between parties that have agreed on it. Output is always written
with the 'T', so it is valid either way round.

The 'T' and the 'Z' are read in either case, so
2024-06-15t14:00:00z names the same instant as the upper-case
form shown above. RFC3339 permits that outright. Output uses an
upper-case 'T' and spells the offset out, +00:00 rather than Z,
so a column of results is one width whatever the zone.

An offset is read anywhere from -23:59 to +23:59, since that is
what a file may carry; -o/--offset writes only -12:00 to +14:00,
the range real zones use. So a record whose offset is outside
that converts, and asking for the same offset as a target does
not.

A fraction of a second is read to nine digits, and more than
nine is parse_err rather than a value quietly shortened. It is
written back at three, six or nine digits, whichever first
covers what the value holds: .5 is written .500 and .1234 is
written .123400. A fraction of nothing (.0) is dropped.

A seconds field of 60 is read only where a leap second can be,
which is the last second of a UTC month, and one instant the
world over rather than a reading on any particular clock. All
three of

|    2016-12-31T23:59:60Z
|    2017-01-01T05:29:60+05:30
|    2016-12-31T18:59:60-05:00

are that same leap second, seen from three offsets, and all
three convert.

A :60 anywhere else -- 14:00:60, or 23:59:60 in mid-month, or
the 23:59:60+02:00 that reads like one on its own clock but
lands in the middle of the UTC evening -- is written as
parse_err instead. It is not a leap second there but a damaged
value, most often from a formatter that rounded 59.5 up without
carrying into the minute, and reading it would quietly name the
second after the one it says.

The year is four digits, so 0001 through 9999 and no further. A
conversion whose result would fall outside that writes parse_err
rather than ISO 8601's expanded form (+10000-01-01...,
-0001-12-31...), which is a different format wearing the same
shape and could not be read back. Note a conversion can cross
the boundary without moving the instant:
9999-12-31T23:59:59+00:00 is year 10000 on a +14:00 clock.

The floor is 0001 rather than 0000 because there is no year
zero: the calendar runs 1 BC straight into AD 1, and ISO 8601
writes 0000 for 1 BC only where both sides have agreed to read
it that way. RFC3339 makes no such agreement.

An offset that is not a whole number of minutes cannot be
written either; only -l/--local can produce one, and KNOWN
LIMITS covers it.

Unix epoch (read by -r/--rfc3339, which converts FROM this
format TO RFC3339): the number of whole seconds since
1970-01-01T00:00:00Z, e.g. 1700000000. Negative values (before
1970) are accepted, down to -62135596800, which is
0001-01-01T00:00:00Z, and up to 253402300799, which is
9999-12-31T23:59:59Z. Both ends are the year range above rather
than a limit of their own.

Whole seconds, so a value carrying a fraction is parse_err.
Milliseconds are the usual mistake with log data, and a
millisecond value is a thousand times too large: any date after
1978-01-11T21:31:40Z lands past the ceiling and is written
parse_err rather than a date tens of thousands of years out that
would read like an answer. Divide by 1000 first.

ISO 8601 duration (written by -d/--duration's result column): a
standard way of writing a span of time as P[n]DT[n]H[n]M[n]S,
e.g. PT5S (5 seconds), P1DT6H30M (1 day, 6 hours, 30 minutes),
or P3653D (3653 days, no time component). Zero-valued components
are omitted; an all-zero duration is written as PT0S. Only
days/hours/minutes/seconds are used, deliberately, even for very
large spans -- a year/month unit would require rounding to some
average length (a month is anywhere from 28 to 31 days), and
this tool never approximates a duration.

Sub-second precision is preserved when the source timestamps
carry it, as a fraction of the seconds component (e.g. PT0.999S,
P1DT3H4M5.25S). Use --round-seconds to round to whole seconds.
